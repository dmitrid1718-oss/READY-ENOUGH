No money. No one to call. Start here.

I need help right now.

You do not need an emergency kit or money to take the first step. Choose what you need. These are free information and referral services; assistance and availability vary locally.

Start with a real person: Call 211 Ask for food, a safe place, transportation help, or emergency assistance near you. If 211 cannot be reached, use 211.org while online. If there is immediate danger, call 911.
I'm stranded. My car is out of gas.
  1. Get yourself somewhere safe. If the vehicle is in traffic or in a dangerous location, request roadside or emergency help. For immediate danger, call 911.
  2. Call 211 and ask specifically about emergency transportation, fuel vouchers, or a ride to a safe place. Programs vary; a voucher is not guaranteed.
  3. Ask the gas-station attendant whether a local community assistance program is known. Do not give a stranger your banking credentials or ID photos.
  4. If your phone battery is low, make the essential call first and write down the next steps.
I have no money for food.

Call 211 for a nearby food pantry or free meal. Ask if food is available today and whether transport or identification is required.

USAGov: Immediate food assistance ↗

I have nowhere safe to sleep.

Call 211 and ask for an emergency shelter bed tonight, intake hours, transport options and alternatives if beds are full. If your safety is threatened right now, call 911.

USAGov: Emergency housing ↗

I can't afford a bill or medicine.

Call 211 for local utility assistance, low-cost clinics and medication support. Call your utility or pharmacy directly to ask about hardship programs, payment arrangements or safe prescription alternatives through a clinician. Do not abruptly stop essential medication without medical advice.

I need someone to talk to.

Ask 211 for local community support. If you're in emotional crisis, call or text 988 for free, confidential support. For immediate physical danger, call 911.

Not sure what to say? “I'm in [town]. I have no money and need help today with [food / a place to sleep / transport / medicine]. What is open now, and how do I reach it?”

Water

Water

Do this firstFill a few clean jugs with tap water today and write the date on them.

What you need

  • Stored drinking water
  • A pot and a way to heat it, or plain unscented bleach (6% or 8.25%) and a dropper
  • Clean cloth or coffee filters for cloudy water

How much

1 gallon per person per day, for drinking and basic hygiene. Children, nursing mothers, sick people, and hot weather need more. Pets need water too.

Replace water you bottled yourself every 6 months. Keep store-bought water sealed until its date.

How to use it

  1. Cloudy? Let it settle, then pour it through a clean cloth, paper towel, or coffee filter.
  2. Boil at a rolling boil for 1 minute (3 minutes above 5,000 feet). Let it cool. This is the most reliable method.
  3. Or use bleach. Stir in the amount below. Wait 30 minutes. It should smell slightly of chlorine. If not, add the same amount again and wait 15 more minutes.
Bleach on labelPer gallonCloudy or very cold
6%8 drops16 drops
8.25%6 drops12 drops

What can go wrong

  • Germs vs. chemicals. Boiling and bleach kill germs. They do not remove fuel, heavy metals, salts, or most chemicals. Water that smells of fuel or chemicals, is dark, or has things floating in it: don't drink it.
  • Never use scented, "splash-less," or color-safe bleach.
  • When your utility issues a boil or "do not drink" notice, follow it exactly. They know what's in the water; this page doesn't.

Prepare before an outage: Local Emergency Radio Guide — find and test frequencies, learn radio rules, and save a one-page PDF to your phone.

Already have this? Use it. Clean soda bottles and juice jugs work for storage (not milk jugs, which break down). Your water heater tank holds clean water: turn off its power or gas first, then drain from the bottom valve. The back tank of a toilet works for washing if you don't use cleaning tablets.

Food

Food

Do this firstLook in your pantry. Count how many days of meals you could make without a fridge or stove.

What you need

  • Food your household already eats that needs no fridge and little or no cooking
  • Manual can opener
  • Paper plates, cups, utensils
  • An appliance thermometer for fridge and freezer

How much

Start with 3 days per person. Build toward 2 weeks over time. Include baby food, special diets, and pet food.

Good choices: canned meat, fish, beans, soup, fruit, vegetables; peanut butter; nuts; crackers; cereal; granola bars; shelf-stable milk; comfort food.

How to use it

  1. When the power goes out, keep fridge and freezer doors closed.
  2. Eat from the fridge first, then the freezer, then the pantry.
  3. Put new groceries behind old ones, and use the oldest first.
Doors closedStays cold
Refrigeratorabout 4 hours
Full freezerabout 48 hours
Half-full freezerabout 24 hours

What can go wrong

  • Power out more than 4 hours: throw out fridge meat, poultry, fish, eggs, and milk.
  • Frozen food can be refrozen if it still has ice crystals or is 40°F or below.
  • Throw out anything that smells, looks, or feels off, or feels warm. Never taste food to test it.
  • Never cook on a grill or camp stove indoors.

Already have this? Use it. Most kitchens hold several days of food. Rice, pasta, and oats keep for a long time if you can boil water. A cooler with ice buys the fridge extra hours.

First aid, medicines & access needs

First aid & health

Do this firstWrite down every household member's medicines, doses, allergies, and doctors. Put the list in your kit.

What you need

  • First aid kit
  • Prescription and everyday medicines (pain, allergy, stomach)
  • Glasses, contact solution, hearing aid batteries
  • Backup for powered medical equipment
  • Cooler and ice packs for refrigerated medicine

How much

Enough medicine to last through your largest stage. Ask your doctor or pharmacist whether you can keep a small extra supply.

For powered equipment (oxygen, CPAP, a power wheelchair), know how many hours your backup battery lasts.

How to use it

  • Bleeding: press hard with a clean cloth and keep pressing. Call 911.
  • Learn the basics once: a Red Cross first aid and CPR class or a Stop the Bleed course takes a few hours.
  • Plan around people: infants, older relatives, and anyone with a disability or mobility needs. Who checks on them? How would they leave?
  • If someone relies on powered medical equipment, tell your electric company. Many keep a list.

What can go wrong

  • Medicine that touched flood water should be thrown out, even in a closed bottle. Exception: a lifesaving medicine with no replacement, if it looks unchanged, until you can replace it.
  • Insulin or other refrigerated medicine got warm: check the label or call a pharmacist. If it is life-sustaining and you have no replacement, the FDA says you may keep using it until you get more.

Already have this? Use it. A clean T-shirt makes a pressure bandage. A cooler from the garage keeps insulin cool. Your phone can store photos of prescription labels.

Communication

Communication

Do this firstAgree on one out-of-state person everyone in the household will check in with.

What you need

  • Battery-powered or hand-crank radio, ideally a NOAA Weather Radio with tone alert
  • Phone, charger, charged power bank
  • Printed contact sheet
  • Whistle to signal for help

How much

One radio per household. One power bank per phone, kept charged. Two meeting places: one right outside your home, one outside your neighborhood.

How to use it

  • Text instead of calling. Texts often get through when calls don't.
  • Keep emergency alerts turned on in your phone settings.
  • Save battery: low power mode, dim screen, close apps.
  • Use 911 for life-threatening emergencies only. Use 211 for shelters, food, and local help.

What can go wrong

  • A dead phone means lost numbers. Keep them on paper.
  • Rumors spread fast. Trust local officials, the National Weather Service, and your utility.
  • This site is not an alert service. Sign up for official alerts.

Already have this? Use it. Your car radio works when the power is out. Most phones already receive Wireless Emergency Alerts.

Before the blackout

Your Phone Can Navigate Without Internet

Do this first Open your phone's Compass app and download an offline map of your home area and likely evacuation routes before service fails. Practice using both with airplane mode turned on.

Compass: works offline

Most modern phones have a magnetic sensor. The native compass can point north without Wi-Fi, mobile data or a cellular signal. Keep it away from car bodies, magnets, speakers and large metal objects; recalibrate if directions seem wrong. Some phones lack a compass sensor.

GPS: works offline too

GPS satellite signals do not require internet or a cell tower. The first location fix can take longer without network assistance, and reception can be poor indoors, underground or among tall buildings. Check your coordinates while outdoors with a clear sky view.

Maps: download first

Map detail, search and routing often require downloaded data. Save your region in your preferred maps app, check that it remains available, and keep a separate paper map. Not every offline map includes walking routes or live road closures.

Know the limitations

Battery, satellite reception and magnetic interference all matter. Phone directions may use magnetic north or true north depending on settings. A website cannot reliably read every phone's compass sensor. Do not rely on one device or assume an untested app works offline.

Five-minute phone check

  1. Open your phone's built-in compass and learn to orient it away from metal objects.
  2. Find where your phone or maps app displays latitude and longitude; write down a sample coordinate and learn how to read it to a rescuer.
  3. Download offline maps for your home, work, and possible travel or evacuation routes.
  4. Turn on airplane mode and verify the compass, saved map and position display work without a connection (GPS may need time to acquire satellites).
  5. Carry a charging option and a paper map or basic magnetic compass as backup.

Remember: No internet does not automatically mean no compass or GPS. Prepare the maps while online; use them offline.

Power & light

Power & light

Do this firstCharge every power bank in the house and put flashlights where you can find them in the dark.

What you need

  • A flashlight per person, extra batteries
  • Power banks for phones
  • Battery-powered or battery-backup carbon monoxide alarms near sleeping areas

How much

Enough to charge each phone once a day for your stage. Compare the power bank's capacity (mAh) to your phone's battery size; the bank should be at least as big for one full charge.

How to use it: compare your options

OptionGood forKeep in mind
Power banksPhones, radios, small lightsCheapest start. Recharge every few months.
Your carCharging phonesRun it outdoors only, never in a garage, even with the door open.
Portable battery stationLights, Wi-Fi, CPAP, short fridge runsNo fumes, safe indoors. Check its watt-hours against what you need.
Solar panelRecharging power banks or a stationSlow when cloudy. Pairs with a battery.
Fuel generatorLarger loads for longerOutdoors only, 20+ feet from doors, windows, vents.

What can go wrong: carbon monoxide

CO has no smell or color. Symptoms feel like the flu: headache, dizziness, weakness, upset stomach, vomiting, chest pain, confusion. People who are asleep can die before they notice.

  • Never use a generator, grill, charcoal, or camp stove indoors or in a garage.
  • If you suspect CO, get everyone into fresh air and call 911.
  • Candles cause house fires. Use flashlights.

Already have this? Use it. Headlamps from camping, solar garden lights brought inside at night, the car's USB ports (outdoors).

Simple things that work

Practical devices

Do this firstUse tools you already have. Test them on an ordinary day, not during an emergency.

Proven low-tech helpers

  • Hand fan: personal cooling when electricity is out. It does not lower room temperature or make extreme heat safe.
  • Manual can opener: access shelf-stable food without power.
  • Clean water container with a tap: dispense stored safe drinking water without pumps. The container does not purify water.
  • Whistle and small mirror: simple ways to attract attention when calling for help.
  • Battery-free daylight: use windows when outdoor conditions are safe; keep a flashlight for darkness.

On the invention bench — not emergency equipment

Anomaly Vent 001: a paper-clip-simple passive airflow attachment being explored by Anomaly Works. The question: can one inexpensive shape improve ventilation driven by wind or temperature differences?

Experimental only. No validated design or performance rating exists yet. Do not rely on it for shelter ventilation, smoke or toxic-gas protection, fallout protection, or safe operation of fuel-burning equipment.

Safety first. During wildfire smoke, chemical releases, or radiation emergencies, follow official shelter-in-place instructions rather than opening vents. Never run generators, grills, or fuel-burning stoves indoors; ventilation gadgets cannot make this safe.

Heat, cold & shelter

Heat, cold & shelter

Do this firstFind your nearest cooling or warming center. Call 211 or check your county's website.

Heat: what to do

  • Drink plenty of fluids. Wear loose, light clothes.
  • Cool showers, shade, rest in the hottest hours.
  • A fan alone won't cool a body in extreme heat.
  • Never leave people or pets in a closed car.
  • Check on older adults, kids, and neighbors.

Cold: what to do

  • Pick one room. Close doors, cover windows, gather everyone.
  • Layers, hats, socks, blankets. Change out of damp clothes.
  • Keep time outdoors short.
  • Let faucets drip to help keep pipes from freezing.

What can go wrong: know the signs

ConditionSignsDo this
Heat strokeHot, red, dry skin; fast strong pulse; confusion; passing outCall 911. Move to shade, cool with wet cloths or ice on neck, armpits, groin.
Heat exhaustionHeavy sweating, pale, cramps, weak, dizzy, headache, nauseaMove somewhere cooler, loosen clothes, sip water. Call a doctor if it lasts over an hour.
HypothermiaShivering, exhaustion, confusion, fumbling hands, slurred speech, drowsinessGet them warm and dry. Seek medical help right away.
FrostbiteNumbness; white or grayish-yellow skin; firm or waxy skinGet somewhere warm. Seek medical help. Don't rub the area.

When home isn't safe: no heat in freezing weather, no cooling in extreme heat for someone at high risk, smoke, flooding, or structural damage. Go to a shelter, cooling center, or someone else's home.

Already have this? Use it. Sleeping bags, winter coats, a tent pitched indoors for kids (it holds warmth), damp towels for cooling.

Sanitation

Sanitation

Do this firstPut a box of heavy-duty trash bags and some kitty litter next to your kit.

What you need

  • Heavy-duty trash bags
  • A 5-gallon bucket with a lid (or your toilet bowl)
  • Kitty litter, sawdust, or another absorbent
  • Soap, hand sanitizer, wet wipes
  • Bleach or disinfectant

How to use it

  1. Turn off the toilet's water valve and flush out the water in the bowl.
  2. Line the bowl or bucket with two trash bags.
  3. Add litter or another absorbent after each use.
  4. Tie off full bags. Keep them in a covered can away from kids and pets.
  5. Wash hands every time. No running water: wipes and sanitizer.

What can go wrong

  • Sewer lines may be damaged even if water still runs. Follow local instructions before flushing.
  • Never mix bleach with ammonia or other cleaners.

Already have this? Use it. Any bucket, kitchen trash bags, the cat's litter.

Stay or leave

Leaving home

Do this firstLearn whether your area has evacuation zones, and which one you're in.

What you need

  • A grab-and-go bag per person
  • Meds, documents, phone and charger
  • Pet carrier, leash, food, records
  • A place to go: friends or family outside the area, a hotel, or a public shelter

How to use it

  • Keep the gas tank at least half full when trouble is possible.
  • Know two routes out. Follow the routes officials give.
  • Plan for anyone who can't drive or needs help moving.
  • If told to leave, go. Bring pets.
  • Tell your out-of-state contact where you're headed.

Stay or go?

Follow local officials. If they say evacuate, leave. If they say shelter in place, stay inside, away from windows, with a radio on. If you're not told either way and your home is safe, staying is usually fine.

What can go wrong

  • Don't drive through flood water. One foot of moving water can sweep a car away.
  • Don't go back until officials say it's safe.
  • Only turn off gas if you smell it or are told to. A utility worker must turn it back on.

Radiation emergencies

Radiation

Do this firstRemember three words: Get inside. Stay inside. Stay tuned.

Get insideThe nearest sturdy building. Basement or the middle, away from doors and windows. Brick or concrete is best.
Stay insideAfter a nuclear explosion, at least 24 hours unless officials say otherwise. Radiation levels drop quickly in that time.
Stay tunedRadio, TV, phone alerts. Officials will say when to leave and where to get checked.

What you need

  • Battery or crank radio
  • A spare set of clothes per person
  • Trash bags for contaminated clothes
  • Sealed food and bottled water, medicines
  • Wet wipes or cloths for washing

The basic idea

Three things lower exposure: less time near the source, more distance from it, and more shielding (walls, earth, concrete) between you and it.

You may have 10 minutes or more after a nuclear explosion before fallout arrives. Use it to get to the best shelter nearby.

How to use it

  1. Close up. Shut and lock windows and doors, close fireplace dampers, and turn off fans, A/C, and forced-air heat that pull in outside air.
  2. Bring pets inside.
  3. If you were outside: carefully take off your outer layer of clothing. That can remove up to 90% of radioactive material. Don't shake it out. Put it in a plastic bag away from people and pets.
  4. Wash. Warm shower with lots of soap, shampoo your hair. Don't scrub or scratch. Keep cuts covered. No shower: wash hands and face at a sink, or wipe exposed skin with a damp cloth. Gently blow your nose and wipe eyelids, eyelashes, and ears.
  5. Food and water inside the building are safe. Sealed containers that were outside are fine after you wipe them. Don't eat garden produce or uncovered outdoor food until officials say so.
  6. Pets: brush their coats, then wash with soap and water if you can.

What can go wrong

  • Potassium iodide (KI) is not a radiation pill. It only protects the thyroid, and only from radioactive iodine. Take it only when public health officials or your doctor tell you to. CDC says it won't help after a nuclear explosion, so don't leave shelter to look for it.
  • Iodized salt and iodine supplements are not substitutes and can be harmful in large amounts.
  • Don't use household cleaning wipes or hand sanitizer on your skin to remove contamination.
  • Don't leave shelter early to find family. Stay where you are and use your out-of-state contact.

Live near a nuclear power plant?

Every U.S. plant has a planning zone of about 10 miles where people may be told to shelter, evacuate, or take KI, and about 50 miles where officials may restrict food and water. If you're inside the 10-mile zone, learn your zone or sector name and how your county will alert you.

01Neighborhood lifeboat

Ask: Am I in immediate danger? What single useful action can I take? Who should I contact? Do that first. In a life-threatening emergency, move to safety and call emergency services.

Context, not a prediction

Why now?

90days, reported

On September 30, 2026, the Pentagon issued a memo launching FORTRESS America, a program to make major U.S. military bases able to keep running without civilian power, fuel, water, and supply lines. Reporting puts the target at at least 90 days. It's a Defense Department initiative, not a law passed by Congress, and it covers military bases, not households.

We're not reading anything into it. We just like the logic: if the professionals plan to be self-reliant for 90 days, a household can plan for three, then two weeks.

The far more likely reasons to need this page are ordinary: storms, wildfires, ice, floods, a long outage, a water main break. Being ready for those means you're mostly ready for anything.
