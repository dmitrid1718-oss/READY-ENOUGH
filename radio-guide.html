Your Local Emergency Radio Guide

Find it now. Save it. Have it when you need it. When the internet goes down, your radio may still work. But you need to know where to tune. Find and test frequencies before an emergency. Save this guide as a PDF on your phone and print a paper copy.

Use these official and established sources while online: NOAA station lookup · SAME county codes · Ham repeaters · FCC rules. Check your county emergency-management and local broadcaster websites for emergency AM/FM stations; confirm any GMRS repeater details with its operator.

The ZIP is for your reference: this page does not automatically populate or verify frequencies. Coverage varies; test reception ahead of time.

On a phone, use Print or Share and choose Save as PDF where supported. Open the saved file with internet off to confirm it is accessible. Keep paper backup.

Which radio do you need?

  • AM/FM receiver: local broadcast news and Emergency Alert System messages. Car and battery-powered radios work offline. Ordinary AM/FM radios do not receive NOAA weather channels.
  • NOAA Weather Radio: weather and alert broadcasts on seven channels, 162.400–162.550 MHz. SAME-capable models can be configured for county warnings.
  • Scanner / wideband receiver: compatible receive-only services. Many public safety systems are digital or encrypted and may not be monitorable.
  • GMRS / amateur radio: two-way communication, subject to equipment and licensing rules. Program frequencies, repeater offsets and tones before a crisis; repeaters may lose power.

How to use it

  1. Before an outage: test your primary and backup channels, label presets, charge or replace batteries, confirm local repeaters, and agree when family members will listen.
  2. During an outage: start with NOAA and local broadcast stations. Try backup frequencies if one is silent; silence is not an all-clear.
  3. For two-way radio: listen before transmitting, speak briefly and clearly, use only authorized frequencies, and keep emergency channels available.
  4. For reception: safely move near a window or outside, adjust the antenna, and try another location.
  5. For life-threatening emergencies: call 911 when available. Radio channels may not be monitored.

How to signal a rescue aircraft

Make yourself easy to find without putting rescuers at risk. Call emergency services when possible. Tell them your location, the number of people, injuries and the signals you plan to use.

  • Signal mirror or small reflector: in sunlight, use brief reflected flashes toward a distant rescue aircraft. Practice aiming before an emergency; never use a laser pointer.
  • Bright ground marker: lay out contrasting fabric, a bright tarp or a large SOS on open ground. Secure loose material against rotor wash; stay clear of landing zones.
  • Flashlight at night: flash an ordinary light in a repeated pattern where a search team can see it. Avoid intense beams into pilots' eyes.
  • Smoke: only use purpose-made emergency smoke signals according to their instructions, where lawful and safe. Never start a fire to make smoke, especially during wildfire risk or near aircraft operations.
  • Whistle and movement: three repeated whistle blasts and deliberate waving may help rescuers nearby identify you. Conserve energy and shelter when needed.

Do not: aim lasers at any aircraft, ignite brush or improvised smoke devices, obstruct a landing area, or approach a helicopter unless the crew directs you. Follow rescuers' instructions.

Rules: Receiving is different from transmitting. Routine US GMRS and amateur transmissions generally require the applicable FCC license. Do not transmit on NOAA broadcasts, police/fire, aviation, or other restricted channels. Emergency exceptions are limited and service-specific; follow current FCC rules.
